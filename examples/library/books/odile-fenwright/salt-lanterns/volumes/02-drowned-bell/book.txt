Chapter 1


The Morning After

By breakfast the whole of Ellamere was talking about the bell.

The fishwives on the quay said it was the old chapel bell from the drowned quarter, the streets that had slid into the harbor in a landslip before anyone now living was born. The guild apprentices said it was a ship's bell on a wreck. The children said it was a ghost, which was the most popular answer.

Aldous Fenn, the harbormaster, came down from the harbor office in his green coat with its brass anchor buttons, his moustache waxed into two stiff points, and announced that there was no bell, that anyone spreading tales of a bell would be fined, and that the harbor was closed for repairs to the sea wall.

"Repairs to what?" Nell said to Tamsin. "The wall's fine."

Tamsin pushed her spectacles up her nose. "Then he's closing the harbor for something else."





Chapter 2


The Harbormaster

That night Nell stayed out after her round, crouched in the shadow of lantern forty with her coat pulled over her knees.

At the second watch, a cart came along the wall without lights. Two of Fenn's dockhands unloaded sacks from the guild's own salt store and carried them down the slipway, to a boat waiting below the wall with its oars muffled in rags.

Nell knew those sacks. She had filled half of them herself. Without that salt there would be no brine for the lanterns by midwinter.

She told Corwin in the morning. He listened, braiding and unbraiding the copper wire in his beard, and said only, "Fenn's a thief. He's not the bell."

"How do you know?"

But Corwin had already gone up the Tidewatch steps, and he did not come down again until dusk.





Chapter 3


Below the Wall

The tide went out farther than usual on the day after the full moon, and Tamsin had worked out exactly when.

They climbed down the iron rungs at the foot of the Tidewatch at noon, when the Murk was thinnest, and walked out across the wet black stones of the old drowned quay. Weed hung from the bollards. There were doorways in the cliff-face below the town, choked with sand, where houses had once had front steps.

Tamsin looked through her fogglass and went still. "There's a line in the fog that isn't a current. It's straight."

It was a rope. Someone had tied it to a bollard at the end of the quay with a lamplighter's knot, and it ran out across the shallows and into the white, taut, as if something at the far end were holding on.

Nell knew the knot. She had learned it at six years old, standing on the guild steps.





Chapter 4


The Line

They came back at dusk with two salt lanterns on poles and followed the rope into the Murk.

Inside, the fog was not white but grey-gold where their lanterns lit it, and it moved around them like a crowd making room. Sounds came strangely: the sea from the wrong side, gulls that were not there. Tamsin read the currents through her fogglass and Nell kept one hand on the rope.

Halfway out they found Fenn's boat, run aground on a sandbar, loaded with the guild's salt. A dockhand was huddled in it, terrified, his own lantern long dead. Fenn had been selling the salt inland, he told them, sacks at a time, for years. That was all. A thief, as Corwin had said.

They gave him a lantern and pointed him back along the rope. Then they went on.





Chapter 5


The Bell-Ringer

At the end of the rope stood the drowned chapel, its roof gone, its tower still standing in the shallows with water lapping at the belfry stair.

Someone was in the belfry. A man, thin as a heron, his skin grey-pale as the fog itself, his hair long and colourless. He held the bell-rope in both hands, and when he saw their lanterns he let it go and shielded his eyes.

"Nell," he said. His voice was cracked with disuse, but she knew it at once. It was her father's voice.

Tobin Harrow had not drowned. The Murk had taken him in, ten years ago, and kept him, and changed him. He could not leave it now; the salt light hurt his eyes and his skin. But he had found the old bell, and learned that when he rang it, the fog drew back from the town. He had been ringing it every bad night for ten years, the bell-ringer no one could see.

Behind them, a lantern came bobbing along the rope. Corwin Ashby climbed the belfry stair, leaning on his ash pole, and would not meet Nell's eyes.

"I knew," he said. "I found him in the second year. He made me promise not to tell you. He didn't want you to see him like this."





Chapter 6


The Mother Lamp

They could not bring Tobin out of the Murk. They tried, at the edge, with the lanterns shaded, and he staggered and went grey to the lips, and Nell pulled him back into the fog herself.

So they made another plan.

Tamsin drew a new chart, marking a lantern post at the end of the drowned quay, where the light would reach into the fog but not too far: lantern one hundred, the first new lantern in Ellamere in a century. Corwin persuaded the guild to build it, and Fenn, who was in a great deal of trouble, persuaded the harbor office to pay for it.

On the first night it was lit, Nell climbed the Tidewatch and worked the brine valve of the Mother Lamp while Corwin sat beside her, and out in the white, the bell rang once, as if in answer.





Epilogue


Nell Harrow is master of the Lamplighters' Guild now. She still lights the round herself, one to one hundred, and she still wears the grey coat.

Every night, when the last lantern is burning at the end of the drowned quay, she stands beside it for a while and talks into the fog. Every night, out in the white, something rings like a bell.
