Prologue


The night the lanterns failed, the sea wall of Ellamere was crowded with people who had come to watch the fog arrive. They called it the Murk. It came every night from the Grey Reach, thick and pale as wet wool, and it did not drift with the wind the way honest fog does. It crept. It turned at corners. It found open doors.

Ninety-nine salt lanterns stood along the wall to hold it back, and that night the far ones guttered out one after another, from the harbor mouth inward, as if someone were walking along the wall and pinching each wick.

Tobin Harrow took a fresh lantern and a coil of rope and went out along the wall to relight the farthest one. His daughter, six years old, stood at the guild steps holding the end of the rope. She felt it pay out through her hands for a long time. Then it stopped paying out, and it went slack, and when the lamplighters hauled it in, the end was cut clean.

Out in the white, very faintly, something rang like a bell.





Part One


The Lamp

Salt keeps the dark out. Everyone in Ellamere knows that.





Chapter 1


The Ninety-Nine

Ten years later, Nell Harrow lit the lanterns herself.

She was sixteen, short and wiry, with copper hair cut close to her skull so it would not catch fire, and a shiny burn scar across the back of her left hand from her first winter as an apprentice. She wore her father's grey wool coat, which still came down past her knees, its brass buttons stamped with the guild's lantern.

The salt lanterns were tall glass cylinders on iron posts, each one numbered on a little enamel plate. Inside, a wick soaked in brine burned with a blue-white light that made the wet stones of the sea wall shine like fish scales. Ordinary fire did nothing to the Murk. Salt fire made it flinch.

Nell worked her way out from lantern one, by the guild hall, to lantern ninety-nine at the harbor mouth, trimming each wick and topping up the brine. Behind her came Master Corwin Ashby, tall and stooped, his white beard braided with a length of copper wire. His left eye was milky and blind. He carried a lighting-pole of black ash wood with a brass hook at the end, and he tapped every post with it as he passed, as though counting them.

"Ninety-nine," he said at the end, as he always did. "Ninety-nine and the Mother Lamp. Never fewer."





Chapter 2


Brine and Wick

The guild cellar smelled of salt and lamp-black. Nell spent her mornings there, dipping new wicks into the brine vats and hanging them to dry on wooden racks like rows of grey tails.

Tamsin Reed came down the cellar steps with her map case banging against her hip. She was a cartographer's apprentice, a head taller than Nell, with dark brown skin and black hair in dozens of small braids tied off with blue thread. Her fingers were always stained with ink, and her round spectacles had been mended at the bridge with twine.

"The edge moved," Tamsin said, before hello.

She unrolled a chart of the harbor on the drying table and weighed its corners with wick spools. She had been mapping the Murk for a year, standing on the wall at dusk with her fogglass, a brass tube with a lens of smoky crystal that showed the fog's slow currents as faint lines, like grain in wood. On her chart, the line marking how far the Murk reached each night had crept inward by the width of a street.

"It's leaning on the wall," Tamsin said. "Like it's pushing at a door."





Chapter 3


The Dark Lantern

Lantern sixty-one was dark when Nell reached it that evening.

Not out. Dark. The wick was wet and whole, the brine reservoir full, but the glass was cracked in a spiderweb, and the cracks had started from the inside. Beyond the wall, the Murk had come right up to the stones there, close enough that Nell could have reached into it.

She did not reach into it. She set down her satchel, unscrewed the cracked chimney, fitted a spare, and struck a salt match. The new flame took slowly, sputtering blue, and the fog drew back from the wall a step at a time, sullen, like a dog sent out of a kitchen.

Corwin came up behind her and looked at the broken glass for a long time with his good eye.

"Don't tell the harbormaster," he said. "Don't tell anyone. Not yet."





Part Two


The Fog

What the light holds back, it also keeps.





Chapter 1


The Tidewatch

At the end of the sea wall stood the Tidewatch, the oldest lighthouse on the Grey Reach: a squat tower of black basalt with a lamp room of salt-crusted glass on top. Two hundred and twelve steps spiralled up inside it. Corwin climbed them with Nell at his heels, stopping on every landing to breathe.

The lamp room held the Mother Lamp, a salt lantern as tall as a man, its brine fed from a copper tank by a slow drip. Its light went out over the harbor mouth in a wide pale fan, and where it touched the Murk, the fog thinned like breath on a cold window.

"Your father used to sit up here with me," Corwin said. "He thought the Murk was listening. He used to talk to it."

"What did he say?"

Corwin was quiet for a while. "Mostly he asked it to give things back."





Chapter 2


Tamsin's Map

Tamsin spread her map across the floor of the Tidewatch lamp room. Over months, she had drawn the Murk's edge in pencil for every night, one faint line on top of another, so that the harbor was ringed with grey like the inside of a tree.

"Look at these nights," she said, and tapped three lines that bulged outward, away from the town. "The fog went back. A long way. All at once."

On each of those nights, in the margin, she had written the same word in her small neat hand: bell.





Interlude: The Keeper's Log


From the lamp-room log of the Tidewatch, in Corwin Ashby's hand, dated nine years before:

Heard the bell again tonight, out past the harbor mouth, a little after the second watch. The Murk drew back from the wall while it rang. Lanterns steady. Told no one. Will tell no one.





Chapter 3


The Long Night

The storm came in from the Grey Reach on the last night of autumn, and the lanterns began to fail.

They went out the way they had ten years before, from the harbor mouth inward. Nell ran the wall with a satchel of salt matches while the rain drove sideways, relighting what she could. Tamsin stood at the top of the Tidewatch with her fogglass and shouted the gaps down to her through a speaking-trumpet.

By midnight only the Mother Lamp held. Corwin's hands shook too badly to work the brine valve, so Nell worked it, and Tamsin fed the copper tank from buckets hauled up the two hundred and twelve steps.

At dawn the storm blew itself out. The Murk lay over the harbor, quiet and white. And from somewhere beneath the water, under the old drowned quay, a bell rang three times.
